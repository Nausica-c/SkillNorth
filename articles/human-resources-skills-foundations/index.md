Human resources connects an organisation's goals with the people who do the work. HR responsibilities can include recruitment, onboarding, training, performance processes, employee relations, retention and organisational culture. The exact scope varies by country, organisation size and sector.

A beginner should first understand the purpose of each HR process and the responsibilities that come with handling employment information. HR work often involves sensitive personal data and decisions that must comply with local employment law and organisational policy.

## The core areas of human resources

### 1. Recruitment and selection

Recruitment begins with understanding the role and the skills required. A clear job description helps applicants assess fit and helps the organisation compare candidates fairly. Selection processes should use relevant criteria and consistent evaluation. Avoid assumptions based on protected characteristics or irrelevant personal information.

### 2. Onboarding and employee support

Onboarding helps a new employee understand their responsibilities, workplace processes, tools and support contacts. Good onboarding is more than paperwork: it helps people understand how work is organised and where to ask questions.

### 3. Learning and development

HR and managers may identify skills gaps, plan training and support internal development. Useful learning objectives should connect to work requirements and be followed by opportunities to practise. Attendance alone does not show that a new capability has been developed.

### 4. Performance and feedback

Performance processes should make expectations clear and provide a fair opportunity to discuss progress, support and improvement. Managers need to use specific evidence and document decisions appropriately. Local law and organisational policy matter, especially in formal performance or disciplinary matters.

### 5. Retention and workplace culture

People's experience of work can be affected by role clarity, management quality, development opportunities, workload, inclusion and trust. HR can help organisations listen to employees and identify patterns, while recognising that survey results or turnover numbers need context.

### 6. Confidentiality and compliance

HR teams may handle sensitive information about pay, performance, health, family circumstances and employment status. Access should be limited to people with a legitimate reason. Keep records secure and follow applicable data-protection and employment requirements.

## How HR is changing

Digital systems and AI tools can support parts of recruitment, administration and learning. They also create risks around bias, transparency, privacy and oversight. Human judgement remains essential when decisions affect people's opportunities and working lives. The [International Labour Organization's 2026 report on skills in the age of AI](https://www.ilo.org/publications/changing-landscape-skills-age-ai) highlights the continuing importance of cognitive and socio-emotional capabilities alongside digital and AI skills.

## A practical learning exercise

Choose a fictional role and draft a simple onboarding checklist. Include what a new employee needs to know in the first week, which systems they need access to, who is responsible for each step and where sensitive information should be stored. Then review the checklist for clarity, accessibility and data minimisation.

This exercise helps you understand HR process design without handling real employee records or giving legal advice.

## Common questions

### Do I need a degree to start learning HR?

A degree may be required or preferred for some roles, while other entry points may value practical experience and relevant training. Requirements differ by country and employer. Review job descriptions in the market where you plan to work.

### Is HR only about recruitment?

No. Recruitment is one part of a broader function that may include onboarding, employee support, learning, performance, workplace relations and organisational development.

### Can an online course qualify me to give employment-law advice?

Do not assume that it can. Employment law is jurisdiction-specific and changes over time. Formal advice may require qualified legal professionals or appropriately trained specialists in the relevant jurisdiction.
